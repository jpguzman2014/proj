# Legal Pages

Five pages, linked from the footer on every page, reachable in one click.

## Privacy policy

Required by the GDPR in the EU, the UK GDPR, the CCPA and CPRA in California, PIPEDA in
Canada, the Privacy Act in Australia, and the LGPD in Brazil. It is triggered by
analytics alone, so nearly every site needs one.

Must state: who the controller is with real contact details; what data is collected,
including data collected automatically such as IP addresses and cookie identifiers; why,
and the lawful basis for each purpose; who it is shared with, naming the processors;
where it goes if transferred outside the origin region; how long it is kept; the rights
available and how to exercise them; and how to complain to a regulator.

Name the actual processors. "We may share data with third parties" is not compliant.
List Stripe, Google Analytics, Mailchimp, Vercel, and whoever else genuinely receives
data.

## Terms and conditions

The contract. Cover who you are, what the service is, acceptable use, payment terms,
intellectual property ownership, liability limits, termination, governing law and
jurisdiction, and how changes to the terms are notified.

Liability limits that are too aggressive are struck out wholesale in consumer contexts
in many jurisdictions. Consumer statutory rights cannot be waived by a term.

## Cookies policy

Either its own page or a clearly delineated section of the privacy policy. Tabulate
every cookie: name, provider, purpose, duration, and category (strictly necessary,
functional, analytics, or marketing). Explain how to change the choice later, and make
that route actually work.

## Refund policy

Publish it before taking payment. In the EU and UK, distance selling gives consumers a
14-day right to withdraw, with specific exemptions for digital content that the customer
must expressly acknowledge before download. Many US states require a posted policy, and
its absence can imply a right to refund.

State: the window, the conditions, who pays return shipping, the refund method and
timing, and any exclusions, exclusions being the part most often omitted.

## Real business details

The EU E-Commerce Directive and the UK Companies Act require identification of the
trader. Publish the legal entity name, which is often not the trading name, a registered
address that is a real postal address, an email address, the company registration
number, and the VAT or tax number where registered.

A contact form on its own does not satisfy the email requirement in the EU. Sole traders
still publish a name and an address at which they can be served.
